Most of this site tells you what the harness does. This page tells you what we
were thinking when we built it, and what we still argue about.

## Where we think the harness stands

Before we settled on this shape, we studied the harnesses we respect and
measured ours against them. With the models available today, we think ours
compares well. We are proud of that.

We are also careful with the word "today". Models improve every few months.
Each improvement moves the line between what a harness must supply and what a
model brings on its own. Some of the scaffolding we built this year will be
unnecessary next year. We would rather write that down than let this site imply
we found the answer. We found an answer that works today, and we keep checking
whether it still does.

## A catalog that gives your agent eyes

The part of this harness we defend hardest is the
[catalog](/standards/catalog). The obvious version would have been a
linter: measurable rules, pass or fail, done. Design does not work that way. A
harness that pretends otherwise enforces whatever a script can count and loses
the rest.

So the catalog ranges the way design judgment ranges, from deterministic to
abstract. One end holds rules a script can measure: contrast floors, target
sizes, and steps on the type scale. The other end holds calls that need
judgment: whether a page reads as generated, or whether motion tells the truth
about what changed. Every standard declares which kind it is, how it gets
checked, and what waiving it costs. That range is what gives your agent eyes: a
way of reading a screen that holds the measurable and the judged at once.

We also built the catalog to be read. Every standard is a page a designer can
open and argue with. The file the checks run against is the file this site
renders, so the two cannot drift apart. A standard that only tooling can read is
one your team will ignore, and your team would be right to.

## Why DESIGN.md lives in your repo

We could have kept every product's design language in one central place. We
chose the opposite: the
[design language skill](/harness/skills#the-design-language) interviews you,
reads your code, and writes a `DESIGN.md` that stays in your product's repo.

We chose it because your context is not our context. Every team ships under
different constraints: a different audience, a different codebase, and a
different history of decisions that made sense at the time. A central file would
flatten those differences or go stale trying to track them. A file in your repo
evolves the way the rest of your product does, through pull requests, by the
people closest to the work.

It also keeps the choice of design system yours. If your team already has one,
`DESIGN.md` points at it. If you want a different one, adopt it without asking
us. The catalog still holds the floors that protect users: contrast,
target sizes, and copy that says what happens. Everything above those floors
belongs to your team.

That is the trade we are trying to make: consistent where inconsistency would
hurt users, flexible where your product knows better than we do.

## Where the harness came from

This site is about design, and design is what the harness ships. It did not
start there. Before the design harness we built an engineering one. Its review
loops, issue skills, and repo conventions taught us how agents behave inside a
codebase. The design harness inherits that shape: everything is a file in a
repo, checkable and versioned.

We are designers and engineers building together, and neither word alone covers
the team. The closest description is system thinkers who love craft. We care how
a standard reads and how it runs. We want our products to stay worth using at the
speed and scale that agents make possible.

## What we expect to change

We built the structure to hold more than it holds today. The catalog can carry
another design system's rules. The skills are a set we curate, and we expect to
add some and retire others. If a better way of working with agents appears, the
harness should be able to absorb it. If it cannot, we will discard the harness
before we discard the better way.

Writing that sentence is cheap, so here is what meaning it costs us: re-reading
our own decisions when a new model ships, retiring work we were fond of, and
telling you when something here is out of date instead of quietly leaving it up.
What stays constant is the job underneath. We adjust how we support teams
working with AI, and we keep that support within reach of people who do not
write code every day.

If this page stops being true, the fix is to change the harness, not the page.

The TransformX product design team
