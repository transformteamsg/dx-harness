# Research Plan Builder

This skill builds a complete user research plan — the alignment document that sets every research project up for success. A research plan manages expectations, gives the team a shared understanding of what the research is for, and forces clarity before a single question is written or a single participant is recruited.

## Why a research plan matters

Without one, research projects suffer from: scope that shifts mid-study, stakeholders expecting different outcomes than what was delivered, sessions that go off-topic because goals weren't clear, and findings that don't help anyone make decisions. A research plan is not bureaucracy — it's the difference between research that lands and research that gets filed away.

When you start using research plans consistently, studies become more focused, stakeholders come to readouts already understanding what to expect, and findings lead to action because they're directly tied to what people needed to know.

---

## Before writing the plan: the intake process

The biggest efficiency win in research planning is an intake document. Instead of chasing stakeholders through meetings to get context, send them a form first. This gets version one of the research plan written without a single unnecessary meeting.

Use intake when:
- Research requests come in via Slack, email, or ad-hoc conversations
- You're working with stakeholders who are new to research
- You want to cut planning time from two weeks to three days

**Intake document questions to ask:**

*Background*
1. What led you to request research support now?
2. What phase of your project are you in — discovering new information, testing a concept, or something else?

*The big picture*
3. What impact will this research have on your team?
4. How will this research impact other teams or the organization?

*Deep dive into the research need*
5. What level of support do you need — recruitment, guide writing, moderation, analysis?
6. What are your questions for this project?
7. What groups of customers do you want to learn about?
8. What customer problem or need are you trying to solve?
9. How do you know this is a customer problem or need?
10. What will you do with the insights? Is there someone ready to act on them?
11. When do you need insights to move forward, and why that date?
12. Any other relevant context?

*Looking back*
13. What previous research, analytics, or data supports the solution you've designed?
14. What important unanswered questions remain after reviewing past research?
15. Please include links to any prototypes or concepts.

**Important:** A business problem is NOT the same as a user problem. If the request comes in as "not enough people are signing up" or "drop-off rate is high" — that's a business metric, not a research question. Before writing any plan, translate business problems into user problems using these questions:
- Is this actually a customer problem?
- Are there technical issues that could be causing this instead?
- Why would customers care about this?
- How could this be impacting customers?
- Would solving this help customers? If so, how?
- Have we heard about this in previous research?

A user problem is almost always a business problem too. But a business problem is not automatically a user problem — and researching the wrong thing leads to findings that don't help anyone.

## What you need from the user first

Before writing the plan, collect (from the intake doc or conversation):
1. **The research request** — what triggered this project?
2. **The business problem and the user problem** — separately
3. **What decisions this research needs to support** — what will change based on what you find?
4. **Who the stakeholders are** — who needs to be involved, informed, or consulted?
5. **What's already known** — previous research, analytics, assumptions
6. **Rough timeline expectations** — when do they need insights?

## The 12 components of a research plan

### 1. Project background

2-4 sentences on what the research is about, why it's happening now, and what's at stake — from both the business and the user perspective. Include relevant business KPIs or metrics that could be impacted.

Do NOT write a business problem as a user problem here. Name both separately.

**Template:**
"[Team/project context]. From the business side, we are hoping to impact [KPI/metric]. From the user side, we want to understand [user problem/experience]. This research will help us [decision the team needs to make]."

**Example:**
"The search team was approached by senior management who want navigational search made a priority in the Merchant Portal. There isn't much understanding of what users think of search functionality or how they would use it. We want to delve deeper to uncover how implementing navigational search would impact the business and users. Business objectives include increased time on platform and improved retention."

---

### 2. Internal stakeholders

List everyone involved and their role. Use the RACI model when helpful:
- **Responsible** — who is doing the work
- **Accountable** — who has final authority over successful completion
- **Consulted** — people with unique insight the team will draw on
- **Informed** — people not directly involved but kept updated

---

### 3. Hypotheses and assumptions

List separately — they are different things.

**Hypotheses** — ideas or concepts the team is unsure about, open to being wrong about. These drive exploration.

**Assumptions** — ideas the team believes are true. These are more dangerous because people treat them as settled. Surfacing them is critical — assumptions that go unexamined lead to research designed to confirm rather than learn.

Note: in user research, we never "validate" assumptions — we explore and evaluate them. Sample sizes are too small for validation.

---

### 4. Research statement and goals

This is the most important section.

**Research statement** — one or two sentences describing what you're trying to learn at a high level. Use this model:

"We want to better understand how users [think about / make decisions on / interact with] [subject of research / product] in order to [create / improve] [product/website/app/service]."

**Research goals** — 2-3 specific areas to explore that map to the research statement. These are not questions. They are areas you want to have enough data on by the end to feel confident making decisions.

Maximum 3 goals. More than 3 increases scope and makes depth impossible.

**Common generative research goals:**
- Discover people's current processes/decision-making about [research subject] and how they feel about the overall experience
- Learn about people's current pain points, frustrations, and barriers about [current process/tools] and how they would improve it
- Understand what [research subject] means to people — how they define it and why it matters to them

**Common evaluative research goals:**
- Evaluate how people are using / interacting with [product/website/app/service]
- Uncover the current tools people use to [achieve goal] and their experience with those tools
- Uncover pain points or confusion people encounter when interacting with [prototype/experience]

**Goal-writing questions to ask stakeholders:**
- What information do you need at the end of this project? Why?
- What are the top three questions you need answered?
- What decisions do you want to be able to make by the end?
- What is the ideal outcome?

**Mad-lib exercise for stakeholders (highly effective for getting clarity):**
"I need [information] to understand [proposed research goal] to make [decision] that will impact [team/organizational goal]. By the end of the study, I need [ideal outcome]."

**Bad vs. better goal examples:**

Bad: "Understand why participants order food."
Better: "Understand the end-to-end journey of how and why participants choose to order food online."
Why: The bad version is too broad and feels like a problem statement, not a goal. It has no direction or boundaries.

Bad: "Find out how to get participants to order food online."
Better: "Uncover participants' thought processes and prior experiences behind ordering food online."
Why: "How to make someone do something" is not a researchable question. You can't ask users to solve your business problem.

Bad: "Find out why people use [competitor] to order food."
Better: "Discover the different tools participants use when deciding to order food, and how they feel about each."
Why: Don't pre-load the question with a specific competitor unless comparative analysis is the explicit goal.

---

### 5. Success criteria

How will you know this project succeeded? Success criteria should be measurable and tied to the research goals — not product metrics, but research outcomes.

Examples:
- Identified and clearly articulated the top 5 pain points
- Uncovered and clearly articulated the top 3 unmet needs
- Have enough information to create 2-3 concepts to test
- Successfully ran an ideation workshop with the team

Ask: "What would make you and your team sit at the end of this project and say 'that was a success'?"

---

### 6. Methodology

Name the method(s) and briefly explain why they're the best fit for the goals. If multi-phase, describe each phase.

**Generative research:** 1x1 interviews, diary studies, contextual inquiry
**Evaluative research (qualitative):** qualitative usability test / experience feedback session, concept testing
**Evaluative research (quantitative):** quantitative usability test, tree testing, first-click testing, surveys with metrics
**Hybrid:** generative first, evaluative follow-up

Match methodology to what you're trying to learn. If the team needs to understand why users behave a certain way → qualitative. If they need to measure how many and how fast → quantitative. If they need to test a mid-fidelity design → qualitative usability test. If they need to benchmark a live product → quantitative usability test.

---

### 7. UX metrics (evaluative studies only)

If the study is quantitative, list the metrics you'll track:
- Time on task
- Task success (pass/fail or 3-point scale)
- Number of errors
- SEQ (Single Ease Question)
- UMUX / SUS
- Other (SMEQ, Confidence, etc.)

Skip this section for generative studies.

---

### 8. Participants and recruitment

**Participant criteria:**
Start by asking: what behaviors, habits, goals, and experiences do participants need to have to give you meaningful data? Brainstorm criteria by asking:
- What behaviors are you looking for?
- Have they needed to use the product? In what timeframe?
- What goals are essential for the user to have?
- What habits are you targeting?

Then write a screener survey based on these criteria.

**Recruitment section should cover:**
- Number of participants (don't forget to over-recruit by 20%)
- Number per segment, if applicable
- How you'll recruit (panel, agency, LinkedIn, HotJar, etc.)
- Session format: remote or in-person
- Session length
- Participant demographics
- Incentive amount
- Link to screener survey

**Sample size guidelines:**
- Generative / qualitative: 10-15 per segment for saturation; minimum 5
- Qualitative usability test: 7-10 per segment
- Quantitative usability test (moderated): 5 per segment minimum; 15+ unmoderated
- Benchmarking: 25+ per segment
- "Per segment" always means per distinct audience type — never total across all segments

---

### 9. Deliverables and next steps

What will come out of this research? Be specific about format and why that format was chosen.

Common deliverables: research report, topline summary, annotated prototype, journey map, mental model diagram, research readout presentation, affinity diagram.

Also: name any planned workshops (ideation, co-design, synthesis). Qualitative research almost always needs an ideation session after findings are shared — build this into the plan from the start.

Don't just list deliverables — explain why each one was chosen over alternatives. "A journey map" without a reason often ends up unused.

---

### 10. Approximate timeline

Stakeholders need timeline expectations set early. Include buffer of 1-2 weeks. Frame as approximate.

**Phases to include:**
- Planning and scoping
- Recruitment
- Conducting sessions (can overlap with recruitment)
- Analysis and synthesis
- Report-building
- Share-outs / readouts
- Workshops

**General timeline guidelines:**
- Generative research: 4-6 weeks
- Evaluative research: 2-4 weeks
- Hybrid (generative + evaluative): 3-5 weeks
- Add 1-2 weeks per additional segment

---

### 11. Discussion guide / task script

Can live in the research plan or in a separate document. If included here, this is the interview guide or task script — introduction, questions/tasks, and wrap-up.

Keep a note here linking to the guide even if it's separate, so the plan stays a single reference document for the whole project.

---

### 12. Resources

Links to everything relevant:
- Intake document
- Screener survey
- Raw data (recordings, notes)
- Synthesis board
- Report / presentation
- Prototypes or concepts
- Previous relevant studies

This section makes the plan useful for people joining the project late and for your own future reference when building case studies.

## Researcher reminders to always include

- Never start writing interview questions before the research goals are agreed on — questions should directly serve goals
- A business problem is not a user problem — name both separately and make sure the goals address the user problem
- Maximum 3 research goals per study — more than 3 makes depth impossible
- Always over-recruit by at least 20%
- The research plan is a living document — update it as the project evolves, especially the resources section
- If stakeholders haven't seen the plan, the project isn't ready to start — alignment before recruitment, always
- Use the mad-lib exercise with stakeholders who struggle to articulate what they need: "I need [information] to understand [goal] to make [decision] that will impact [org goal]."
