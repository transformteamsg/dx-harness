# Research plan components

Guidance for each component of the plan, in plan order. Component 7, UX metrics, lives
in [ux-metrics.md](ux-metrics.md), and only an evaluative or hybrid study reads it.

### 1. Project background

Write two to four sentences on what the research is about, why it is happening now,
and what is at stake, from both the business and the user perspective. Include the
business KPIs or metrics the research could affect.

Name the business problem and the user problem separately. Do not write a business
problem as a user problem.

Template:

```
[Team or project context]. From the business side, we hope to affect [KPI or metric]. From the user side, we want to understand [user problem or experience]. This research will help us [decision the team needs to make].
```

Example:

```
The search team was approached by senior management, who want navigational search made a priority in the Merchant Portal. There is little understanding of what users think of search or how they would use it. We want to find out how navigational search would affect the business and users. Business objectives include more time on the platform and better retention.
```

### 2. Internal stakeholders

List everyone involved and their role. Use the RACI model where it helps:

- **Responsible**: who does the work
- **Accountable**: who has final authority over successful completion
- **Consulted**: people with unique insight the team will draw on
- **Informed**: people not directly involved but kept updated

### 3. Hypotheses and assumptions

List them separately, because they are different things.

- **Hypotheses**: ideas the team is unsure about and open to being wrong about. These drive exploration.
- **Assumptions**: ideas the team believes are true. Surface them, because an unexamined assumption leads to research designed to confirm rather than learn.

Never write that the research validates an assumption. User research explores and
evaluates assumptions, because its sample sizes are too small for validation.

### 4. Research statement and goals

**Research statement**: one or two sentences on what you want to learn at a high level.
Use this model:

```
We want to better understand how users [think about / make decisions on / interact with] [subject of research or product] in order to [create / improve] [product, website, app, or service].
```

**Research goals**: two or three specific areas to explore that map to the research
statement. A goal is not a question. It is an area you want enough data on, by the
end, to make decisions with confidence.

Write three goals at most. More than three widens the scope and makes depth impossible.

Common generative research goals:

- Discover people's current processes or decision-making about [research subject], and how they feel about the overall experience
- Learn about people's current pain points, frustrations, and barriers with [current process or tools], and how they would improve it
- Understand what [research subject] means to people: how they define it and why it matters to them

Common evaluative research goals:

- Evaluate how people use or interact with [product, website, app, or service]
- Uncover the tools people use to [achieve goal] and their experience with those tools
- Uncover the pain points or confusion people meet when they interact with [prototype or experience]

Questions to ask stakeholders when you write the goals:

- What information do you need at the end of this project? Why?
- What are the top three questions you need answered?
- What decisions do you want to be able to make by the end?
- What is the ideal outcome?

Where a stakeholder struggles to say what they need, give them this fill-in sentence:

```
I need [information] to understand [proposed research goal] to make [decision] that will impact [team or organisational goal]. By the end of the study, I need [ideal outcome].
```

Weak and stronger goals:

| Weak | Stronger | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Understand why participants order food. | Understand the end-to-end journey of how and why participants choose to order food online. | The weak version is too broad and reads as a problem statement. It has no direction or boundaries. |
| Find out how to get participants to order food online. | Uncover participants' thought processes and prior experiences behind ordering food online. | "How to make someone do something" is not a researchable question. Users cannot solve your business problem for you. |
| Find out why people use [competitor] to order food. | Discover the different tools participants use when they decide to order food, and how they feel about each. | Do not name a specific competitor unless comparative analysis is the explicit goal. |

### 5. Success criteria

State how the team will know the project succeeded. Make each criterion measurable and
tie it to the research goals. These are research outcomes, not product metrics.

Examples:

- Identified and clearly stated the top five pain points
- Uncovered and clearly stated the top three unmet needs
- Have enough information to create two or three concepts to test
- Ran an ideation workshop with the team

Ask the stakeholders: "What would make you and your team say at the end of this
project that it was a success?"

### 6. Methodology

Name the method or methods, and say briefly why each fits the goals. For a study with
more than one phase, describe each phase.

| Study type | Methods |
| --- | --- |
| Generative | One-to-one interviews, diary studies, contextual inquiry |
| Evaluative, qualitative | Qualitative usability test or experience feedback session, concept testing |
| Evaluative, quantitative | Quantitative usability test, tree testing, first-click testing, surveys with metrics |
| Hybrid | Generative first, then an evaluative follow-up |

Match the method to what the team needs to learn:

- Why users behave a certain way -> qualitative
- How many, and how fast -> quantitative
- A test of a mid-fidelity design -> qualitative usability test
- A benchmark of a live product -> quantitative usability test

### 8. Participants and recruitment

**Participant criteria**: decide which behaviours, habits, goals, and experiences a
participant needs to give you meaningful data. Find the criteria with these questions:

- What behaviours are you looking for?
- Have they needed to use the product? In what timeframe?
- Which goals must the user have?
- Which habits are you targeting?

The researcher writes the screener survey from these criteria, outside this plan.

The recruitment section covers:

- Number of participants, over-recruited by at least 20%
- Number per segment, where the study has segments
- How you will recruit: a panel, an agency, LinkedIn, Hotjar, or another channel
- Session format: remote or in person
- Session length
- Participant demographics
- Incentive amount
- A link to the screener survey

Sample sizes:

| Study | Participants |
| --- | --- |
| Generative or qualitative | 10 to 15 per segment for saturation, five at minimum |
| Qualitative usability test | Seven to 10 per segment |
| Quantitative usability test | Five per segment at minimum when moderated, 15 or more when unmoderated |
| Benchmarking | 25 or more per segment |

"Per segment" means per distinct audience type, never the total across all segments.

### 9. Deliverables and next steps

State what the research will produce. Be specific about each format, and say why you
chose it over the alternatives. A journey map with no reason often ends up unused.

Common deliverables: a research report, a topline summary, an annotated prototype, a
journey map, a mental model diagram, a research readout presentation, and an affinity
diagram.

Name any planned workshops: ideation, co-design, or synthesis. Qualitative research
almost always needs an ideation session after the findings are shared, so plan it from
the start.

### 10. Approximate timeline

Set timeline expectations early. Include a buffer of one to two weeks, and call the
timeline approximate.

Phases to include:

- Planning and scoping
- Recruitment
- Conducting sessions, which can overlap with recruitment
- Analysis and synthesis
- Report-building
- Share-outs and readouts
- Workshops

Typical durations:

| Study | Duration |
| --- | --- |
| Generative | Four to six weeks |
| Evaluative | Two to four weeks |
| Hybrid | Three to five weeks |
| Each additional segment | One to two weeks more |

### 11. Discussion guide or task script

The guide can live in the plan or in a separate document. In the plan, it is the
interview guide or the task script: the introduction, the questions or tasks, and the
wrap-up.

Where the guide is a separate document, link to it here, so the plan stays the single
reference for the whole project.

### 12. Resources

Link everything relevant:

- Intake document
- Screener survey
- Raw data: recordings and notes
- Synthesis board
- Report or presentation
- Prototypes or concepts
- Previous relevant studies

This section keeps the plan useful for people who join the project late, and for the
researcher's own later case studies.
